## Decisions for the review dashboard

1. **Use blacks, not broad gray panels.** Proposed implementation targets—not sampled reference colors—are `#000000` for the canvas, `#050505` for the working surface and `#0a0a0a` only where separation is necessary. Use fine neutral borders, alignment and spacing for structure. Keep white text crisp; reserve muted text for genuinely secondary information. No blue/green cast, oversized gray cards or broad glow.
2. **Build identity around reviewing.** Use a restrained path/selection/annotation motif tied to the actual workflow, then repeat it in the brand mark, selected state and empty state. Use a considered type hierarchy and tabular numbers. Do not paste in satellites, terminal numerals, arbitrary textures or a generic AI sparkle.
3. **Let actionable records lead.** Give the main list enough width to show the record name, actual state and next supported action. Give incomplete or blocked work a clear visual distinction. Use a quieter secondary rail only for information that helps choose what to open next.
4. **Require a job for every chart.** If the real data supports it, show review volume over time or unresolved review age, labelled with the period and units. Selecting a meaningful point/bar should expose the matching records. Prefer direct labels and a small number of semantic colors. Omit the chart when there is no decision or reliable dataset behind it; do not replace it with a decorative pie, gauge or invented trend.
5. **Make the states feel finished.** Selected filters, selected records, empty results, upload progress and errors need specific labels and usable recovery actions. This detail is more transferable from Yuri's work than adding more cards.

The authored quality comes from a consistent vocabulary and information shaped around a real question. A dashboard loses that quality when every statistic becomes an interchangeable card and every visualization could belong to another product. ClearPath should be recognizable through the review workflow, disciplined black surfaces, intentional typography and precise states—not through copied decoration.
